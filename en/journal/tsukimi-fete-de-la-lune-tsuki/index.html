Tsukimi moon viewing: Japan’s autumn moon, and my painting Tsuki

By William San, on 23 October 2026

Today, Friday 23 October 2026, is jūsan’ya in Japan, the thirteenth night. People who sat out under the autumn moon on 25 September look up at it a second time. Those two evenings make up tsukimi, Japan’s moon viewing, and they gave one of my paintings its name: Tsuki, the moon.

In September, at the art3f fair in Monaco, I introduced the painting on camera with whatever words came to me. In Japan, September is the month of the moon: people settle down, they sit, they look at the moon, and they do nothing else but look at the moon. In the video, right after the word “tsukimi”, I add “I think”. I have checked since.

Looking at the moon, and nothing else

Tsukimi means “looking at the moon”. The main evening is called jūgoya, the fifteenth night of the eighth month of the lunar calendar. On our calendar it falls between mid-September and early October, never on the same date two years running. On the stand I said “September”, which is right most years but not all of them. In 2025, jūgoya fell on 6 October.

This year the National Astronomical Observatory of Japan put it on 25 September, although the moon was not actually full until the 27th, at 1.49 am Tokyo time. The lunar calendar starts each month on the day of the new moon. So the festival moon and the astronomers’ full moon do not always land on the same night.

The custom came from China, where people mark the Mid-Autumn Festival. It is said to have settled in Japan during the Heian period, between the late eighth century and the twelfth. Aristocrats would watch the moon reflected on the water from boats, and write poems.

Dango, pampas grass and a rabbit

Today people set out a small table of offerings by a window or on a veranda. On it go tsukimi dango, white rice dumplings, round like the full moon; as nippon.com explains, the shape is the point. Then a few stems of susuki, Japanese pampas grass, whose plumes are said to stand in for ears of rice not yet harvested. Taro from the harvest is so common on these tables that this moon is also called imo meigetsu, the potato moon.

Then there is the rabbit. When people in Japan look at the dark patches on the full moon, they see a rabbit pounding rice to make mochi.

The thirteenth night

The second evening is almost unknown in Europe. Jūsan’ya falls on the thirteenth day of the ninth lunar month, roughly a month after the first: this year, tonight. The moon is not quite round yet. This second festival is said to have been born in Japan, whereas the first came from China. It is also known as the chestnut moon, or the bean moon, after the offerings of the season.

Watching only one of the two moons was once thought to bring bad luck. It was called kata-tsukimi, half a moon viewing.

A woman holding the moon

Tsuki is an oil on canvas, 60 × 60 cm. A young woman in a blue kimono, in profile, her hair tied with a ribbon, holds a full moon in her cupped hands, as if it had been brought down to her. All the light starts there. It catches her lowered face and her fingers, then fades into the brown dark.

On the stand I put the idea like this: I wanted to make a painting that refers to the month of September, still in a slightly chiaroscuro style, like Akari, my woman with the lantern. There is no table of offerings on the canvas, and no autumn grasses. There is a woman alone at night, doing nothing but look at the moon. Except that here she is holding it.

That is what I mean when I say I give a shadow to the images of Japan. The moon is the only source of light in the picture, and whatever it does not touch stays in the dark.

From Monaco to Luxembourg

Tsuki went to two art3f fairs. The first was in Monaco, at the Chapiteau de Fontvieille, from 11 to 13 September: I was 22 and exhibiting in Monaco for the first time. On camera I introduced her as “one of the paintings I have left”.

The second was in Luxembourg, at Luxexpo The Box, from 2 to 4 October, on the Atelier Joconde stand I share with my father, Le Yack, and my brother, Neo Pablo. On the Friday, an hour before opening, she was on the wall. On the Saturday her spot was empty. She sold during the fair and now belongs to a private collection. I wrote about that weekend in my Popsan wall at art3f Luxembourg.

Between the two fairs, on 25 September, came jūgoya. Tonight is the second moon. In Japan, people sit down and look at it. They do nothing else.
