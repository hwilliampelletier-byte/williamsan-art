Limited edition print vs original painting: how to choose

By William San, on 26 October 2026

Koi Look, a wide-open eye turned into a pond where koi carp swim round the iris, exists in two forms. As an original, it is an oil on canvas, 80 × 80 cm, the only one in the world, at €2,600. As a limited edition print, signed by hand, it starts at €300 for 40 × 40 cm. Same image, same title, and yet they are not the same object.

I sell both, under my two signatures: Popsan and Medsan originals, and Medsan prints. So here is what really separates a limited edition print from an original painting. It comes down to four things: rarity, signature, certificate and price.

Rarity: one or twenty

The original is a one-off. When it goes, it is gone for good. In September, at the art3f fair in Monaco, Akari found a buyer, and so did Kingyo, which I introduced on the stand as my favourite piece. Tsuki followed in Luxembourg in October. All three are now in private collections.

A print, on the other hand, belongs to a limited edition. With Medsan, each image is editioned at 20 copies per size: Kidneyugi, for instance, exists in 20 copies at 40 × 40 cm and 20 at 60 × 60 cm. Once the twentieth copy of a size is sold, there is no twenty-first.

Signature and certificate

Every Medsan print is signed and numbered by hand. In art editions, the convention described by the Tate is to write the number as a fraction: 7/20 means the seventh copy in an edition of twenty. The first figure changes from one copy to the next; the second never moves. That is the one to look at.

An original needs no number: my signature is enough. On Tsuki, in the photograph at the top, “Popsan” is written in white in the bottom right-hand corner.

The original also comes with a certificate of authenticity. It is the document that will follow it all its life, from one owner to the next, and I would keep it with the invoice. For a print, it is the signature and the number, added by hand to each copy, that identify it.

Price

Price follows from all of the above. Here are the price lists, as published.

At Medsan, on med-san.art, prints cost €300 at 40 × 40 cm, €450 at 60 × 60 cm, and €400 at 40 × 60 cm for the two portrait-format images. The price is the same for every image.

The Medsan originals, three oils on canvas at 80 × 80 cm, are €2,600 each.

Popsan originals are oils on canvas, 60 × 60 cm, sold through the Atelier Joconde website, such as Shūden or Le passage de la nuit: €800 unframed, €1,000 in a black floater frame.

Price says nothing about how good an image is: Koi Look is the same at €300 and at €2,600. It says how many people can own it.

How to choose

If you want to be the only person who owns an image, or you are buying for a room that needs a large canvas, go for the original. An 80 × 80 cm takes up a wall, and the prints stop at 60 × 60 cm. If the image matters more than being unique, the print brings it within reach, and a 40 × 40 cm fits above a desk or in a hallway. For a present, a print also lets you pick the work to suit the person receiving it: I wrote a guide on a gift for a new doctor.

What a floater frame is

The term keeps coming up in my prices, so it gets a section of its own. A floater frame, what the French call a caisse américaine, is a wooden frame in which the stretched canvas sits slightly recessed, with a thin gap all round and no glass in front. Seen from the front, the canvas seems to float inside it. It hangs as it is, and the frame covers none of the edges of the image.

On the Atelier Joconde stand at art3f Luxembourg, my three Popsan paintings each hung in a black floater frame, like Tsuki in the photograph at the top. For a Popsan original, the frame takes the price from €800 to €1,000. For a Medsan print, it adds €80 on the 40 × 40 and 40 × 60 cm sizes; the 60 × 60 cm is sold as the canvas alone.

Some of it neither price nor frame can settle. In Monaco, introducing Kingyo, I put it this way: everyone can see things the way they want. What matters is having your own meaning for the work.
