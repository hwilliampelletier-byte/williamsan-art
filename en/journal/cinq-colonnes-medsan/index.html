Spine art: five spines, five stories, from scoliosis to multiple sclerosis

By William San, on 24 October 2026

On 26 September 2026, my painting Chou’s Spine was hanging in Paris, at the Hôtel de l’Industrie, for the exhibition “La Vie en Rose”. It has four sisters. For Medsan I have painted five spines, and each one tells the story of an illness. This is spine art in the most literal sense, and the easiest of the five to read is probably Bonsai Vertebra, which is about scoliosis, the sideways curve of the spine that most often appears while a child is still growing.

I am a painter, not a doctor. The medical facts in this piece come from the NHS and from French public sources: the Assurance Maladie, Inserm and the Haute Autorité de santé.

Bonsai Vertebra and scoliosis

A pine bonsai grows in a wooden bowl in front of a bright window. The lower part of its trunk is not wood. It is a spine, vertebra after vertebra, bending into an S before it turns back into branch, then into pine needles.

The painting starts from one sentence: “the spine can be shaped, but it never quite straightens”. Its page tells the story of a thirteen-year-old girl whose scoliosis is picked up at a school medical check. She is fitted with a brace, told she will grow up with her curve, and for a long time she refuses to look at her own back.

Scoliosis is not bad posture. It is a lasting deformity of the spine in three dimensions, linked to the vertebrae rotating. A postural curve straightens when you change position; a true scoliosis does not. In about 8 cases in 10 the cause is unknown, and it most often starts between the ages of 10 and 15. In France, the Assurance Maladie puts it at 0.5 to 2% of children aged 8 to 15, with girls far more affected than boys. It progresses slowly in childhood, then speeds up at puberty.

When the curve is getting worse in a child who is still growing, the main treatment is a back brace, worn until growth stops. It holds the spine to keep the curve from worsening; it does not straighten it. Surgery is kept for the larger curves. The NHS sets out the options by age.

Bonsai has a style that does not aim for a straight line: moyogi, the informal upright, where the trunk climbs in S-shaped curves. That is the line you find in the painting, a spine that has taken its own shape and still holds the tree up.

Carpal Spine and multiple sclerosis

Carpal Spine is one of the four Medsan originals, an oil on canvas measuring 80 × 80 cm. A golden spine fitted with translucent fins rises towards a burst of light in a night sky, among red and white koi.

Multiple sclerosis is an autoimmune disease of the central nervous system. The immune system mistakenly attacks myelin, the sheath that wraps around nerve fibres and speeds up nerve signals. More than 100,000 people have it in France, about three women for every man, and the first symptoms appear on average around the age of 30. At the outset, in 85% of cases, it comes in relapses: symptoms appear or get worse for more than 24 hours, then ease off partly or completely. It often begins with a problem in one eye, a loss of vision that hurts when the eye moves, called optic neuritis.

The painting’s page tells of a thirty-two-year-old woman who learns she has the disease after a sudden loss of sight. Between relapses her body comes back to her, then leaves again. She lives in that wave.

In Japan the carp stands for perseverance. A legend that came from China tells of a carp that swam up a waterfall on the Yellow River and became a dragon, and Japanese has a phrase for it, koi no takinobori, the carp climbing the waterfall. On the painting’s page, every carp around the spine carries the name of someone who kept going regardless.

Chou’s Spine, the broken heart

Chou’s Spine shows a young woman seen from behind, crowned with red roses. Through her white dress you can see her ribcage and spine, and roses grow between her ribs. It is about broken heart syndrome, which cardiologists call takotsubo cardiomyopathy: after an emotional shock, the heart suddenly changes shape and takes on the form of a Japanese octopus pot. I wrote about its day in Paris in Chou’s Spine at “La Vie en Rose”.

Ayame and Sumi

Ayame’s Spine shows a woman from behind, in a blue flowered kimono, red flowers in her hair. Her spine and ribs are bare. She wears her kimono like armour, and yet it is her back, the most exposed part of her, that she turns to us. Ayame is Japanese for iris.

The painting is about bipolar disorder, a long-term mental illness in which episodes of high mood, mania or hypomania, alternate with episodes of depression, with calmer periods in between.

Sumi Spine is the darkest of the five. Sumi is Japanese black ink, made from soot. The painting is about depression, which a thirty-four-year-old woman describes, on its page, as black ink rising slowly up the spine until it stains everything else. The Greek physicians of the Hippocratic school had a word for it: melancholia, literally black bile.

Why spines

Medsan began with a remark. The person I was with at the time, a medical student, could not find a single anatomical painting she thought was beautiful. I was passionate about medicine. I started putting paintings on Instagram, it took off, and I have not stopped since. I tell how it began in Medsan, the art of medicine.

I am 22, I paint in Orléans, in France, and my aim is to make Medsan the signature of a contemporary artist. I sum up my two signatures in one line: Popsan looks for light in the shadows; Medsan turns medicine into art. A spine, in art, is first of all a shape; in Medsan it is also an illness with a name, and a person with a story.
