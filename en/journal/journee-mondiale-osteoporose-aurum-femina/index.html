World Osteoporosis Day 2026: Aurum Femina, the woman who holds her own skeleton

By William San, on 19 October 2026

A young woman holds the skull of her own skeleton at arm’s length. This is Aurum Femina, the painting I devoted to osteoporosis under my Medsan signature, which pairs anatomy with images from Japan. World Osteoporosis Day 2026 falls on Tuesday 20 October. It is held on that date every year by the International Osteoporosis Foundation (IOF), whose theme for 2026 is “Build, Protect and Strengthen Your Bones for Life”.

Aurum Femina, a painting about osteoporosis

She wears a purple kimono. We see her from behind, her face in profile, pale flowers pinned in her black bun, and she raises both arms towards a skeleton standing beside her, which tilts its head down towards her face. Her eyelids lowered, she holds its skull, one hand under the jaw, the other slipped behind it, out of sight. Fine black cracks run across the cranium. Golden streaks swirl around the two figures, all the way down to the hem of the kimono, where large pale roses bloom.

I gave this painting a story, that of a sixty-two-year-old woman who breaks her wrist in a fall in her kitchen. It comes down to one sentence, hers: “I didn’t know my bones could grow old without me.” The tests that follow reveal osteoporosis that had given no warning. The word frightens her, but her image of herself stays intact. In the painting she still has the face of a young woman, and the skeleton she holds up in front of her is her own.

The title puts two Latin words together, aurum, gold, and femina, woman; I translate it as “the golden woman”. In the painting, gold marks what matters, and one of the golden streaks runs from the kimono right across to the skeleton’s pelvis.

A silent disease

Osteoporosis literally means “porous bone”. Bones grow thin and lose their strength, so an ordinary fall can be enough to break them. The IOF calls it the “silent disease”, because most people do not know they have it until the day a bone breaks. Doctors call this a fragility fracture.

According to the 2026 campaign toolkit, one in three women and one in five men over 50 will have a fragility fracture. And up to 80% of people who have already had a fracture caused by osteoporosis are neither identified nor treated for the disease behind it.

Bone is built before twenty

The first verb, build, is aimed above all at the young. According to the same toolkit, up to 90% of peak bone mass, the most bone you will ever have, is reached by the age of 18 in girls and 20 in boys. Yet many young people are less and less active, the foundation adds, and their nutritional needs are not always met. To build strong bones, it recommends four habits: enough protein and calcium on your plate, exercise that builds muscle and bone, no smoking or heavy drinking, and a healthy weight.

After fifty, protect and strengthen

The other two verbs are mainly for the over-fifties. For the IOF, protecting your bones means knowing your risk factors and guarding against falls, starting at home. Strengthening is for those who have already had a fracture. A first fragility fracture roughly doubles the risk of another, especially in the first year or two, and prompt treatment cuts that risk considerably. As the foundation puts it, it is never too late to strengthen your bones, and all the more so after a fracture.

In the UK, bone density is measured with a DEXA scan. The NHS describes it as quick and painless: you lie on your back on an X-ray table, and it usually takes 10 to 20 minutes. It may be recommended if you have broken a bone after a minor fall or injury.

In France, where I live, the same test is called an ostéodensitométrie. The Assurance Maladie, France’s national health insurer, says it takes under fifteen minutes and involves no injection. It covers 70% of the standard fee in certain cases, including a fracture that happened without a violent impact. It also stresses that treatment for osteoporosis aims to prevent fractures, is long-term and should not be interrupted.

I am not a doctor. The figures and advice in this article come from the IOF, the NHS, Inserm and the Assurance Maladie, and if you want to know how your own bones are doing, the person to ask is your GP.

Questions and answers

When is World Osteoporosis Day 2026?

World Osteoporosis Day 2026 falls on Tuesday 20 October. It is held on 20 October every year by the International Osteoporosis Foundation (IOF), and the theme of its 2026 campaign is “Build, Protect and Strengthen Your Bones for Life”.

What is osteoporosis?

Osteoporosis is a disease of the skeleton, marked by a loss of bone mass and a deterioration in the structure of bone tissue, according to Inserm, France’s national institute of health and medical research. It makes bones more fragile, to the point where a stumble or a light knock can fracture one. Most of the time, as the NHS also notes, it is only diagnosed when that first fracture happens.

How many people have osteoporosis?

Inserm estimates that nearly 4 million people in France had osteoporosis in 2019, or 5.5% of the population. The institute puts the number of fragility fractures that year at 484,000, mostly of the hip, the vertebrae or the wrist. Worldwide, according to the International Osteoporosis Foundation, one in three women and one in five men will have a fragility fracture after 50.

Can osteoporosis be prevented?

The NHS recommends weight-bearing exercise, where your feet and legs carry your weight, such as brisk walking or dancing, along with resistance exercise that works the muscles. It also links smoking to a higher risk of osteoporosis, and the Assurance Maladie in France goes further, saying that smoking makes osteoporosis worse. The International Osteoporosis Foundation stresses a diet with enough calcium and protein. Screening and any treatment are for you and your doctor to decide.
